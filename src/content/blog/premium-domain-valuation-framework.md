Domain pricing feels opaque from the outside. Buyers see a five- or six-figure number with no receipt, and sellers are asked to defend a price that no appraiser fully agrees on. The good news: serious valuations come down to a small set of factors you can evaluate yourself.

## The four pillars of domain value

### 1. Commercial intent of the words

The strongest domains describe a market people already spend money in. Ask three questions:

- Does the exact phrase appear in how buyers search? Type it into a search console or keyword tool and look for real volume around the core term.
- Is there an existing ecosystem of advertisers? Industries with expensive customer acquisition — healthcare, finance, software, legal — support higher domain prices because the name pays back faster.
- Would a company buy this name for defensive reasons as well as offensive ones? A name a competitor could weaponize is worth more than a name nobody needs.

### 2. Length, spelling, and recall

Count syllables, not characters. A seven-syllable phrase that a patient can spell after hearing it once is worth more than a four-letter string that people mishear. Test names out loud: say it once in a meeting, then ask everyone to type it. If more than one person needs a second attempt, the name carries a tax.

`.com` still matters for trust and type-in traffic. Newer extensions can work for community products, but for a company selling to clinics, investors, and regulators, `.com` remains the default expectation.

### 3. Comparables (and how to use them honestly)

Appraisals lean on three sources:

1. **Public sales databases.** Useful for ranges, but skewed upward — big public sales are the visible minority of the market.
2. **Private broker data.** A broker who closes deals in your category can tell you what names actually cleared, which is far more useful than list prices.
3. **End-use economics.** Work backwards: if the name could plausibly lift conversion by even a fraction of a percent over its life, what is that worth to a network with hundreds of locations?

A comparable is only meaningful when the *use case* matches. A short brandable name in an unrelated vertical tells you nothing about a category-defining healthcare domain.

### 4. Risk discount

Every buyer applies a discount for things that could go wrong:

- Trademark exposure in the intended category
- Confusing similarity to an existing brand
- Prior use or history that a diligence process will surface
- Extension risk — a name that only works for one product line

Clean title, documented history, and a transferable registrant record remove most of this discount.

## A simple scoring pass

Before you make or accept an offer, score the name 1–5 on each pillar, weight commercial intent highest, and sanity-check the result against your alternatives. The exercise is less about the arithmetic than about forcing the conversation: **which of these factors is this particular name actually strong in?**

Most negotiations settle when both sides agree on which pillar is in dispute. If the disagreement is about keyword demand, bring search data. If it is about risk, bring the history.

## What a valuation is not

A valuation is not a guarantee of liquidity. Domains are illiquid assets: the buyer pool for any single name is small, and time on market is the hidden cost on both sides. If you are selling, price for the buyer who is already looking. If you are buying, remember that the number on the listing is an opening position, not a market clearing price — [make an offer](/#acquire) that reflects your own economics.
