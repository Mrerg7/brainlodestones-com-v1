A single clinic can name itself after its founder. A network cannot. Once you operate across cities — and plan to operate across more — the name has to carry a promise about scale before anyone reads your website.

## The plural advantage

Plural forms do quiet work for multi-site businesses:

- **They imply more than one.** "Lodestone" is a point of attraction. "Lodestones" is several, coordinated — which is exactly what a franchise, a platform, or a referral network is.
- **They survive expansion.** A name tied to one therapy, one device, or one city forces a rebrand when the roadmap changes. A plural name absorbs new modalities, new geographies, and new product lines.
- **They sound established.** Buyers, investors, and patients read plural names as organizations rather than experiments. In a category where trust is the product, that perception is worth paying for.

The tradeoff is length — plurals add a syllable. That cost is worth it when the added syllable is a common English word, not a suffix bolted onto a coined term.

## Category language vs. coined names

There are two defensible strategies:

1. **Category-defining names** contain the words patients and buyers already use — brain, stimulation, magnetic, TMS, neuro. They win on search comprehension and immediate clarity: a stranger knows what business you are in from the URL alone.
2. **Invented names** win on availability and distinctiveness, then rely on marketing budget to attach meaning.

Networks rarely have the budget of a consumer app. For clinical and B2B brands, a name that carries its own explanation usually outperforms a name that requires a campaign.

## The four tests a network name should pass

**Test 1 — The phone test.** Say the name once on a phone call with background noise. Can the other party type it correctly?

**Test 2 — The expansion test.** Imagine you have added two therapies and three states in three years. Does the name still fit, or does it now mislead?

**Test 3 — The trust test.** Show the name to a clinician and to an investor separately. Does it read as infrastructure, or as a supplement brand?

**Test 4 — The ownership test.** Search the name. Can you own the search results, the social handles, and the exact-match domain? A name you can only rent as `nameapp.io` while someone else holds `name.com` is a structural weakness.

## Names to avoid

- **Location-locked names** unless the business is permanently local.
- **Founder names** for networks — they cap the equity and complicate the exit.
- **Over-specific names** that name one device generation or one procedure.
- **Spellings that need explaining** ("with a K", "no vowels"). Every explanation is a lost visitor.

## The cost of waiting

Premium domains are not manufactured on demand: the specific combination you want either exists in a clean, transferable state or it does not. Rebrands are expensive in direct costs, but the real cost is the accumulated inbound links, reviews, and brand equity you have to rebuild at the new address.

If a name passes all four tests today, evaluate it against [what a domain is actually worth](/blog/premium-domain-valuation-framework/) and move before the category consolidates around someone else's.
