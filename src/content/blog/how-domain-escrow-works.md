Wire a stranger and hope for the best? Nobody serious does that with a five-figure asset. Escrow is what turns a domain purchase from a trust exercise into a process — and once you know the steps, you can spot a bad deal instantly.

## Why escrow exists here

A domain transfer has two halves that cannot happen at the same moment: money moves, and a registrar record changes hands. Escrow sits between them, holding funds until both sides have performed. The seller cannot take your money and vanish; you cannot take the domain and vanish.

The major providers (Escrow.com is the one most brokers reference) also handle the paperwork: transaction terms, fees, the inspection window, and the release conditions.

## The process, step by step

### Step 1 — Agree the terms

Price, payment method, what is included (some deals include brand assets or associated accounts), and the timeline. Everything agreed here appears in the escrow instruction. If a seller resists putting terms in writing, walk away.

### Step 2 — Open and fund the transaction

The buyer funds escrow by wire, ACH, or card depending on the provider and amount. Funds sit in escrow — not with the seller. At this point the seller has no ability to access the money, which is precisely why they can safely start the transfer process.

### Step 3 — Unlock and push the domain

The seller unlocks the domain at their registrar, disables domain privacy if required for the push, and provides the auth code (or initiates a push to your registrar). You approve the incoming transfer and pay your registrar's transfer fee, if any.

Transfers are time-boxed by the registry, so this step usually takes one to five days depending on the registrars involved and whether the domain is within the transfer lock period.

### Step 4 — Inspection

Once the domain lands in your account, you confirm it: correct spelling, correct TLD, expected history, no surprise locks. Escrow programs define an inspection window — typically a few days — during which you can raise a problem before funds release.

### Step 5 — Release

You approve the release, escrow pays the seller minus their fee, and the transaction closes. Registrars also send confirmation emails at each stage, so keep access to the inbox on record.

## What to check before you fund anything

- **Who actually owns it.** The registrant on record should match the person selling it, or there should be documented authority to sell.
- **Transfer status.** A domain recently transferred or newly registered is often under a registry lock; that delays the deal, it does not kill it — but you should know before you fund.
- **Disputes and history.** A short history check surfaces UDRP cases, prior use, and anything a trademark search would flag.
- **Payment channels.** Legitimate sellers work through escrow or a broker you can verify. Pressure to pay by gift card, crypto to an unhosted wallet, or "just send it and I'll transfer after" is the oldest fraud pattern in this market.

## Who pays the escrow fee?

Customary practice varies: sometimes the buyer, sometimes split, occasionally the seller absorbs it as part of a negotiated price. It is a line item worth settling in step 1 rather than discovering at step 5.

## A note on taxes and disclosure

Domain acquisitions can have tax and reporting implications depending on jurisdiction and how the asset will be used. Escrow handles the mechanics of the transaction, not your tax position — for a material purchase, a short conversation with your accountant is cheap insurance.
